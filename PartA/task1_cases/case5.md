**Title:** When a rare simultaneous infection simulates a local extension of a rectal cancer: a case report  
**Authors:** Hicham Baba, Jawad Fassi Fihri, Mohammed Essaid Ramraoui, Ahmed Elguazzar, Ahmed Zeroual, Mohammed Lahkim, Ahmed El Khader, Abdessamad Achour, Rachid El Barni

### Abstract

**Background:**  
Actinomycosis is a rare chronic infection caused by *Actinomyces*. The abdominopelvic localization of this pathology makes preoperative diagnosis extremely exceptional. The following report is the case of a patient treated for adenocarcinoma of the middle rectum associated with mesorectal actinomycosis. The diagnosis of actinomycosis was made postoperatively.

**Case presentation:**  
A 69-year-old Caucasian male patient was admitted for rectal bleeding. Clinical and paraclinical assessment revealed a middle rectum adenocarcinoma with local extension in the mesorectum. The patient underwent an anterior resection of the rectum by laparotomy after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Postoperative follow-up was simple. Pathological study of the specimen noted complete sterilization of the rectal adenocarcinoma and the presence of large foci of suppurative necrosis containing actinomycotic grains in the mesorectum.

**Conclusion:**  
Abdominopelvic actinomycosis is a rare pathology and has therefore rarely been dealt with. This issue can lead to unnecessary and mutilating surgery. We report an exceptional coincidence of rectal adenocarcinoma and mesorectal actinomycosis mistaken for mesorectal extension of the cancer.

### Background

Actinomycosis is a rare chronic granulomatous infection caused by *Actinomyces*, saprophytic bacteria normally found in the oral cavity and digestive tract. Abdominopelvic localization is uncommon and often misdiagnosed as malignancy. Because of its rarity, diagnosis is often made postoperatively on resection specimens, even though treatment typically consists of antibiotic therapy rather than surgery.

This report describes a patient initially believed to have locally advanced adenocarcinoma of the middle rectum with mesorectal invasion, but final histologic examination revealed complete response of the adenocarcinoma and coexisting mesorectal actinomycosis.

---

### Case Presentation

A 69-year-old Caucasian male with well-controlled type 2 diabetes presented with rectal bleeding and rectal syndrome. Rectal examination showed a budding circumferential tumor at 7 cm from the anal margin. Colonoscopy confirmed an ulcerative tumor; biopsy showed a well-differentiated infiltrating Lieberkühn adenocarcinoma.

MRI revealed a circumferential thickening of the middle rectum wall with a heterogeneous exophytic mass infiltrating the mesorectum and abutting the mesorectal fascia. CT scans excluded distant metastases, and laboratory tests showed anemia (10.8 g/dl).

Given the apparent local invasion, the patient received 5 weeks of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Ten weeks later, an anterior rectal resection with low colorectal anastomosis protected by ileostomy was performed via laparotomy to minimize mesorectal disruption.

Histopathology demonstrated complete sterilization of the adenocarcinoma, but large foci of suppurative necrosis containing *Actinomyces* colonies (“actinomycotic grains”) were present in the mesorectum. Ten lymph nodes were negative for metastasis. No adjuvant chemotherapy was indicated, but antibiotic therapy with amoxicillin/clavulanate was initiated.

### Discussion

*Actinomyces israelii* is the primary pathogenic species in humans. Normally commensal in mucosal cavities, it becomes pathogenic after mucosal disruption—often due to surgery, infection, or immunosuppression (for example, diabetes or malignancy). Infection spreads contiguously rather than hematogenously, producing abscesses or pseudotumors that may mimic malignancies.

Abdominopelvic actinomycosis constitutes about 20–24% of cases, usually in the ileocecal region, while rectal involvement is exceedingly rare. Imaging is often nonspecific, resembling solid or cystic neoplasms. Diagnosis typically relies on histologic identification of *Actinomyces* grains, as culture is difficult and requires strict anaerobic conditions.

Treatment is based on prolonged high-dose penicillin therapy (IV 10–20 MIU for 4–6 weeks followed by 6–12 months of oral amoxicillin–clavulanate). Surgical resection is reserved for complications or diagnostic uncertainty.

In this case, the mesorectal actinomycosis was misinterpreted as tumor extension. Only postoperative pathology clarified the dual pathology—chemoradiation-induced tumor regression and a coincidental infectious pseudotumor.

---

### Conclusion

Abdominopelvic actinomycosis remains rare and deceptive, often mimicking neoplastic disease. In this patient, actinomycosis was mistaken for mesorectal extension of rectal adenocarcinoma, leading to unnecessary extensive surgery. Awareness of this condition may prevent future overtreatment through better preoperative recognition and biopsy confirmation.
