### Abstract  
**Introduction:** Vaginal metastasis from colorectal cancer is a rare occurrence, typically found alongside other metastatic lesions. Isolated metastasis is exceedingly uncommon, with only a few cases documented. Vaginal involvement is usually due to direct contiguous spread from the primary tumor.  
**Case presentation:** A 70-year-old African woman with a history of rectal adenocarcinoma treated with chemoradiation and anterior resection developed an isolated metastasis to the lower third of the left vaginal wall two months post-surgery. Biopsy confirmed the lesion as metastatic rectal adenocarcinoma. Imaging excluded other metastases. Surgical abdominoperineal resection with removal of the affected vaginal wall achieved negative margins, and the patient recovered without complications. Adjuvant chemotherapy was initiated and tolerated well.  
**Conclusion:** Isolated vaginal metastasis from rectal cancer is extremely rare. Regular gynecologic examination is essential during colorectal cancer follow-up. Surgical resection with adjuvant therapy is a viable approach for early, isolated metastases.  

---

### Case Presentation  
A 70-year-old African woman, with no family cancer history, presented with perineal discomfort and mild rectal bleeding. Thirty years prior, she had undergone hystero-oophorectomy for a uterine myoma. Examination revealed an irregular posterior rectal tumor 7 cm from the anal margin, while gynecologic exam showed an intact rectovaginal septum.  

**Initial workup:**  
- **Colonoscopy:** Ulcerated, sharply demarcated posterior rectal tumor, 4 cm in height.  
- **Biopsy:** Well-differentiated rectal adenocarcinoma.  
- **CEA:** 6.56 ng/mL.  
- **MRI:** T3N1 lesion with perirectal fat invasion and multiple presacral lymph nodes.  

She underwent neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, followed by anterior resection with total mesorectal excision and temporary ileostomy. Pathology confirmed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma (ypT3N1, R0). After adjuvant chemotherapy and ileostomy closure, she presented two months later with vaginal spotting.  

**Findings:**  
A palpable vaginal mass was found in the lower third of the vagina, invading the rectovaginal septum. Biopsy and immunohistochemistry (CDX2 +, PAX8 –) confirmed colonic-type adenocarcinoma. MRI revealed a 4 cm hyperintense vaginal lesion invading the rectovaginal septum but sparing the colorectal anastomosis.  

**Treatment:**  
An abdominoperineal resection with removal of the posterior and lateral vaginal wall was performed. The specimen showed a 5 × 4.5 cm moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma extrinsically invading the rectal wall up to the muscularis propria; margins were negative. The patient recovered well and began adjuvant chemotherapy.  

---

### Discussion  
Primary vaginal cancers are rare, representing only 2% of gynecologic malignancies, while vaginal metastases from colorectal origin are even less common. The vagina can be secondarily involved via four main mechanisms:  
1. **Direct extension** from adjacent pelvic structures.  
2. **Transcoelomic spread** through the uterus or fallopian tubes.  
3. **Lymphatic dissemination** via iliac or hypogastric nodes.  
4. **Hematogenous spread** via the ovarian plexus and vaginal veins — considered most likely in this case.  

Other reports suggest iatrogenic spread during transvaginal specimen extraction in laparoscopic surgery. Clinical presentation often mimics primary vaginal carcinoma, with vaginal bleeding, discharge, or palpable mass.  

Diagnosis requires biopsy confirmation, with MRI and FDG PET/CT useful for detecting lesions and distant metastases. There is no standardized treatment; surgical resection remains the cornerstone for isolated cases, often supplemented by radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Aggressive multimodal management has shown improved long-term outcomes.  

In this case, surgical excision achieved complete resection, and postoperative chemotherapy was initiated. The patient remained stable during follow-up.  

---

### Conclusion  
Vaginal metastasis from colorectal cancer is exceptionally rare. Routine gynecologic surveillance is critical for early detection. In cases of isolated, resectable metastasis, combined surgical and chemotherapeutic management can yield favorable outcomes.
